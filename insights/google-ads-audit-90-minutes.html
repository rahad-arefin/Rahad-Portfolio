How I audit a Google Ads account in 90 minutes

A practical 90-minute Google Ads audit: conversion tracking, structure, search terms, ads, landing pages, bidding, and a one-page summary owners act on.

THE SHORT ANSWER

Start with conversion tracking, because every other conclusion depends on it. Then check structure and budgets, search terms and negatives, ads and landing pages, and bidding and targeting. Finish with a one-page summary ranking the three biggest money leaks by cost, so the account owner knows exactly what to fix first.

Most Google Ads audits fail in one of two ways. Either they are a 40-page export nobody reads, or they are a quick glance that misses the expensive problems. After running monthly audits across several accounts, I settled on a fixed 90-minute routine that finds the money leaks and ends with something an owner will actually act on.

Before you start

  • Get read-only access. You do not need to change anything during an audit.
  • Compare the last 30 days with the previous 30, and glance at 90 days for trends.
  • Write down the business goal and the target: cost per acquisition, ROAS or cost per qualified lead.
  • Ask what a real conversion is for this business. The answer decides how you read everything else.

Minutes 0 to 15: conversion tracking

If tracking is wrong, every number in the account is wrong, and smart bidding is optimizing toward the wrong thing. I check this first, every time.

  • Primary actions: only the conversions that matter to the business should be primary. Page views and micro-events marked as primary are a classic leak.
  • Duplicates: the same action counted twice, for example a Google Ads tag and a GA4 import firing for one form.
  • Leftovers: conversion actions from an old website, an old funnel or a previous agency setup.
  • Reality check: do conversions in the platform roughly match leads or sales in the CRM or backend?

Minutes 15 to 30: structure, budgets and pacing

  • Which campaigns are limited by budget while others spend freely with weak results?
  • Is brand mixed with non-brand? Brand searches convert cheaply and can hide a weak non-brand program.
  • Do campaign types overlap, so they bid against each other for the same searches?
  • Does the split look like 80% on proven campaigns and a capped share on tests? See the 80/20 budget rule.

Minutes 30 to 50: search terms and negatives

This is where most wasted spend hides. Sort the last 30 days of search terms by cost and look at everything that spent without converting.

  • Irrelevant themes: jobs, free, how-to or competitor terms you never meant to buy.
  • Cross-campaign negatives: when two campaigns serve similar searches, add negatives so each search lands in the campaign built for it. I set these up whenever a new campaign launches next to an existing one.
  • Match types: broad match without strong negatives and good conversion data tends to drift.

Minutes 50 to 65: ads, assets and landing pages

  • Message match: does the ad promise what the landing page delivers, in the same words?
  • Assets: enough headline and description variety, plus sitelinks, callouts and images where relevant.
  • Stale links: after a website or landing page change, sitelinks often still point to retired pages. I have found this more than once, and it is a five-minute fix.
  • The page itself: load it on a phone. Slow loading and long forms quietly raise cost per lead.

Minutes 65 to 80: bidding and targeting

  • Smart bidding needs enough conversion volume to learn. Campaigns with very few conversions may do better on simpler strategies.
  • Are targets realistic compared with recent history, or so tight that the campaign barely spends?
  • Location options: if only people physically in your area can buy, check whether targeting includes people merely interested in it.
  • Ad schedule and devices: look for hours or devices that consistently spend without converting.

Minutes 80 to 90: the one-page summary

The audit is only as good as what happens next. I deliver one page with three parts, and put the detailed findings in an appendix:

  1. Top three money leaks, ranked by estimated monthly cost.
  2. Quick wins this week: negatives, stale links, tracking fixes.
  3. Structural fixes this month: restructuring, bidding changes, landing page tests.

If it does not fit on one page, it will not get acted on.

The checklist

AreaCheckRed flag
TrackingPrimary actions, duplicates, CRM matchMore conversions than real leads
BudgetsLimited campaigns, brand vs non-brandWinners capped, losers uncapped
Search termsCost with zero conversionsIrrelevant themes in top spenders
Ads and pagesMessage match, assets, linksSitelinks to retired pages
BiddingStrategy vs conversion volumeTargets far from history
TargetingLocations, schedule, devicesSpend outside the service area

Key takeaways

  • Audit tracking first; it decides whether anything else is true.
  • Search terms and budget allocation hide most of the waste.
  • Check for stale links after every website change.
  • End with one page ranked by money, not a long export.

Frequently asked questions

How often should a Google Ads account be audited?

A light check of tracking, pacing and search terms every month, and a full audit like this one every quarter or whenever results change sharply.

Can Performance Max campaigns be audited the same way?

Partly. Performance Max gives fewer levers, so focus on conversion goals, asset group quality, search themes, brand exclusions and the landing pages it sends traffic to.

What is the most common problem in a Google Ads audit?

Conversion tracking that counts the wrong things, such as duplicate actions or low-value events marked as primary. It makes smart bidding optimize toward the wrong outcome.
