# Stop judging paid media by CPL: the cost-per-SAL framework

By Rahad Arefin, growth marketing leader · Updated 2026-11-18 · Measurement
Canonical: https://rahadarefin.com/insights/cost-per-sal-framework.html

> **Short answer:** Cost per lead (CPL) tells you what a form fill costs, not what growth costs. Judge paid media on cost per sales-accepted lead (SAL) and cost per booked meeting, and read results through 4-week and 13-week lead cohorts. You will stop scaling channels that only look cheap and start funding the ones that fill your pipeline.

## Why cost per lead misleads

Cost per lead is the most quoted number in paid media and one of the least useful for deciding where money goes. It rewards whatever produces the cheapest form fill. Instant forms, broad audiences and aggressive offers all push CPL down, and they often push lead quality down with it.

I have seen campaigns with a low CPL produce leads that sales could not reach, and campaigns with a CPL twice as high produce the meetings that actually closed. If budget follows CPL, it flows to the wrong place every single week.

*Figure: Funnel from leads to customers showing cost per lead at the top, cost per booking and cost per SAL in the middle, and CAC at the bottom. Where each metric measures. CPL stops at the form fill; cost per SAL and cost per booking measure the stages sales actually trusts. Illustrative funnel, not real data.*

## Measure the pipeline, not the form fill

The fix is to follow each lead through the stages your sales team already uses, and to put a cost on the stages that matter.

| Stage | What it means | Metric to track |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lead | Someone submitted a form or called | Cost per lead |
| Connected | Sales actually reached them | Connect rate |
| Booked meeting | A call or demo is on the calendar | Cost per booked meeting |
| SAL | Sales reviewed the lead and accepted it | Cost per SAL |
| SQL and opportunity | Qualified, with a real deal in play | Cost per SQL, pipeline value |
| Customer | Closed and paying | CAC, MER |

The two numbers I put at the top of every report are **cost per SAL** (ad spend divided by sales-accepted leads) and **cost per booked meeting** (ad spend divided by meetings booked). They are close enough to revenue to be honest, and fast enough to act on.

## Read results in lead cohorts, not calendar weeks

A lead that arrives today may become an SAL next week and a customer in three months. If you count stages by calendar week, this week’s report mixes this week’s spend with last quarter’s leads, and nothing lines up.

Instead, group leads by the week they arrived and follow that cohort forward. I use two windows:

- **4-week cohort:** early quality signals such as connect rate, booked meetings and SALs.
- **13-week cohort:** the deeper picture, showing which leads became opportunities and customers.

Label every week with its date range and keep the latest week in the first row. It sounds small, but it ends most arguments about which numbers are current.

## The CRM counting trap

This is the mistake I see most often, and it quietly ruins funnel reports. Many CRMs report a lead only in its *current* lifecycle stage. Once a lead moves forward, it disappears from the earlier stages.

So if ten leads reached SAL and two of them later became customers, a naive stage report shows eight SALs and two customers. Your best leads look like they skipped the funnel. The fix is simple: **count each stage cumulatively**, meaning the stage itself plus every deeper stage. Then a customer also counts as an SAL, an SQL and an opportunity, which is what actually happened.

## A three-part weekly report that leadership trusts

1. **Channel performance:** spend, leads, CPL, booked meetings, cost per booking, SALs and cost per SAL, by channel and in total.
2. **Lead quality:** connect rate, lost leads and why, so sales and marketing look at the same truth.
3. **Pipeline velocity:** 4-week and 13-week cohorts showing how leads move toward opportunity and revenue.

Every slide carries a spend column and names the report the numbers came from. That way nobody has to ask where a figure came from.

## What changes when you switch

The first month usually brings two uncomfortable decisions. A campaign everyone liked for its cheap leads turns out to produce almost no SALs, so it gets cut. A campaign that looked expensive turns out to produce most of the booked meetings, so it gets more budget. Both moves feel wrong on a CPL report and obviously right on a pipeline report.

## Key takeaways

- Use CPL as an early warning, not as the budget decision.
- Lead with cost per SAL and cost per booked meeting.
- Read results in 4-week and 13-week lead cohorts.
- Count lifecycle stages cumulatively, or your funnel will lie to you.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a sales-accepted lead (SAL)?**
A sales-accepted lead is a lead your sales team has reviewed, reached and agreed is worth working. It sits between a raw lead and a sales-qualified lead (SQL), and it is the first stage that proves a lead is real.

**Is cost per lead useless?**
No. CPL is a useful early warning for creative fatigue or rising auction costs. It just should not be the number that decides where budget goes, because it says nothing about lead quality.

**Why use 4-week and 13-week cohorts?**
B2B and high-ticket pipelines move slowly. A 4-week cohort shows early quality signals, such as connect rate and SALs, while a 13-week cohort shows which leads became opportunities and customers.
